## What we are asking for

We are building a dataset of **Vietnamese scam SMS** for research into SMS fraud detection. We
invite you to send us the **scam, spam and impersonation messages** you receive.

We also ask for **legitimate messages sent automatically by a system** — one-time codes, bank,
telecommunications, school and application notices — so that the dataset holds genuine messages
alongside fraudulent ones for comparison.

**We do not ask for messages written to you by a person.** Not from friends, family or
colleagues, not from anyone who messaged you personally — even if you would be willing to give
them. The reason: somebody else wrote that message, and we cannot ask their permission.

A simple rule so that you need not deliberate: **a message from a personal phone number is not
sent, whatever it says.** Send only messages from a brandname or a service shortcode.

If you are unsure whether a message is one we are asking for — **do not send it**.

## You are free

- Taking part or not **has no effect** on your marks, your results, or your relationship with any
  lecturer. There is no bonus, no priority, nothing.
- The person inviting you **is not anyone who grades you**.
- Submission is **anonymous**: we do not know who sent and who did not. Your declining is
  therefore something we cannot know, and cannot notice.
- You may **stop at any time**, without giving a reason.

## How your messages are handled

There are two ways to send. You may use either, or both.

**Way 1 — copy and paste (preferred).**

1. You run a tool **on your own device**. It automatically masks phone numbers, e-mail addresses,
   long account-like digit runs, your balance and account movements, and names you mark. **Links in a message are kept and published**,
   because they are the most important trace a scam message leaves; only a phone number, e-mail
   address or name inside a link is masked.
2. You **review each message after masking** and then decide whether to send it. If you are unsure
   about a message, drop it.
3. The unmasked original **never leaves your device**.

**Way 2 — send screenshots**, compressed into one `.zip` file, for messages you cannot copy.

1. You crop each image down to the message and the sender's name, and **black out** on the image
   anything that points to you.
2. You review each image before sending it. If you are unsure about an image, drop it.
3. **With this way, the researcher sees the image exactly as you sent it**, including anything you
   did not black out. The researcher turns the image into text, masks the text, and **deletes the
   image** as soon as it has been checked, before the publication date. Images are never published
   or shared.

We store the **masked** message text, the sender name or shortcode as displayed, the month
received, and the label you assign. We do **not** store your number, your name, your class, or
anything identifying you.

The published dataset also carries **no column saying which message came from whom**. The group
of contributors is small, so even a column of codes rather than names could let someone who knows
the group guess — we therefore do not release it.

## Where this data goes

The dataset will be **published openly** alongside a data article describing it, so that other
research groups can reuse it. That means the messages you send, after masking, will be
**public permanently**.

**This means that** once the dataset is published we **cannot remove your messages** — because we
do not know which ones are yours. You can withdraw **before** the publication date; after it, you
cannot. We say this plainly rather than promising something we cannot do.

## Risks

The main risk is a message containing personal information that the filter misses and that you do
not notice on review. That is why the review step matters most, and why we ask only for scam and
system messages — kinds of message that hold nothing private of yours.

Some links carry a code specific to each recipient. If a link holds your name, phone number or
customer code, mask that part or leave the message out.

If you send screenshots (way 2), there is one more risk: the researcher sees everything in the image
that you did not black out. If that makes you uneasy, use way 1, or leave the message out.
